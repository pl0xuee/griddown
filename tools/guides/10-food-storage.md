Two jobs: get through what's already in the house before it spoils, and put the rest somewhere it will still be food in a year.

## The order you eat things

- **Fridge first, 4 hours.** An unopened fridge holds safe temperature about four hours. Cook or eat what's in it today.
- **Freezer next.** A full freezer holds roughly 48 hours, a half-full one about 24. Pack the gaps with bottles of water — a full freezer stays cold far longer, and the ice is drinking water later.
- **Then dry stores.** Rice, pasta, pulses, tins, oil, salt, sugar.
- The danger zone is 5–60 °C (40–140 °F). Perishable food more than 2 hours above 4 °C is suspect, 1 hour if it's above 32 °C (90 °F). Judge by a thermometer, not by smell — most of what makes you ill has no taste and no odour. When in doubt, throw it out; a day's food is cheaper than a week of diarrhoea.

## Dry storage

- Cool, dark, dry, sealed against pests. Shelf life roughly halves for every 10 °C rise, so a cupboard at 15 °C keeps food perhaps twice as long as one at 25 °C.
- In food-grade buckets with mylar liners and oxygen absorbers, white rice, wheat, rolled oats, pasta, dried maize and dried beans keep 10–30 years. Salt and sugar keep indefinitely (sugar needs no oxygen absorber — it sets like concrete).
- What doesn't keep: brown rice, wholemeal flour, nuts, seeds and cooking oil all go rancid in about six months at room temperature, because of the oil in them. Buy small and rotate hard.
- Freeze new grain and flour for 72 hours at −18 °C to kill weevil eggs before it goes into storage.
- Mice chew through plastic. Use metal bins, or buckets somewhere they can't reach.
- Date everything. First in, first out, and a written list of what is where.

## Root cellar and cool store

- Roots (carrot, beet, turnip) want 0–4 °C and 90–95% humidity — damp sand or sawdust in boxes.
- Potatoes: 4–7 °C, dark and ventilated. Colder and the starch turns sweet; light turns them green and toxic.
- Onions, garlic and squash want cool and *dry*, not humid.
- Keep apples away from potatoes and roots — the ethylene they give off makes everything else sprout.

## Preserving

- **Drying** is the cheapest. Fruit to leathery with no beads of moisture when squeezed, vegetables to brittle. Meat for jerky must reach 71 °C (160 °F) before drying, or you preserve the bacteria along with the meat.
- **Fermenting** needs only salt: a 2–3% brine by weight, everything held under the liquid, 18–22 °C. Sauerkraut and salt pickles keep for months and add vitamin C.
- **Salting and smoking**: cold-smoking preserves but needs a tested recipe and curing salts; hot-smoking is cooking, and buys days rather than months. Don't improvise a cure.
- **Canning** is where people die, so be precise:
  - High-acid foods (pH 4.6 or below — most fruit, jams, properly acidified tomatoes, vinegar pickles) can be processed in a boiling water bath.
  - Low-acid foods (vegetables, beans, maize, meat, fish, soups) **must** be pressure canned at 116–121 °C (240–250 °F). A boiling water bath cannot reach the temperature that kills Clostridium botulinum spores. There is no workaround. No oven canning, no dishwasher, no "my grandmother always did it".
  - Follow a tested recipe including its altitude adjustment — both boiling times and pressures increase with elevation.
  - Lids are consumable. Stock them.
- Botulinum toxin does not smell, taste or discolour food. Discard any jar with a bulging lid, a broken seal, spurting liquid, mould or an off smell without tasting it — and discard it properly: boil the sealed jar and its contents for 30 minutes first, or double-bag it, because a bin or a compost heap is exactly where a child, a dog or a pig will find it. Wash everything the spill touched with a 1:10 bleach solution. Boiling home-canned low-acid food for 10 minutes (plus a minute per 300 m / 1,000 ft of altitude) destroys the toxin, and is worth doing as a matter of routine.
