A car is a fast way to travel for as long as there is fuel and the road is clear, and neither lasts. Plan for the car, then plan for its absence.

## Habits that pay once

- **Keep the tank above half.** It is the single cheapest preparation anybody can make. No power means no pumps: filling stations stop within hours of a grid failure.
- Keep the vehicle serviceable: tyres including the spare and the pressure in it, oil, coolant, belts, wipers, and a battery that isn't more than four or five years old.
- Keep paper maps in the car. Every satnav is one dead phone away from useless, and this app is the other half of that answer.

## What lives in the car

Water, warm and dry layers, sturdy boots, a torch with spare cells, a jump pack or leads, a tow strap, basic tools, a tyre plug kit and a 12 V compressor, a first aid kit, spare fuses, duct tape, a hi-vis vest, a folding shovel, traction boards or a bag of grit, cash in small notes, and a phone cable that fits everything.

## Range and driving

- Economy falls apart in stop-start traffic. Steady 80 km/h (50 mph) instead of 110 (70) is worth roughly 20–25% more distance from the same tank.
- Idling burns fuel for nothing. In a queue, switch off.
- Weight and roof loads cost fuel. Take what you need and no more.
- Siphoning modern cars mostly doesn't work — filler necks have anti-siphon valves and screens. **Never siphon by mouth**: a mouthful of petrol in the lungs causes chemical pneumonitis and can kill.
- Don't drive into floodwater. Thirty centimetres (1 ft) of moving water will float a car; 15 cm will take an adult off their feet. You cannot see whether the road is still there.
- In snow, a car is a survivable shelter — but only if you clear the exhaust pipe before running the engine for heat, and run it in short bursts with a window cracked. A blocked exhaust fills the cabin with carbon monoxide.

## Leaving in a vehicle

- Know two or three routes out and where the choke points are: bridges, tunnels, a single mountain pass, a motorway junction. Those are where a mass movement stops.
- Leave early or leave late. The middle is a queue with no fuel in it.
- Travel in daylight, keep the group in one vehicle if you can, and tell somebody at the far end that you're coming.

## When the fuel is gone

- **The bicycle is the answer most people overlook.** 25–60 km a day is unremarkable, it needs no fuel, it carries 20–40 kg with panniers and far more with a trailer, and it's quiet. Keep tubes, patches, a pump, a spare chain, brake pads and cables, and learn to fix a puncture properly before you need to.
- **On foot**: 4–5 km/h on a road, about 3 km/h off it, plus an hour for every 600 m of ascent. Most people carry 20 kg without ruining themselves; well-fitted boots and blister care decide your range more than fitness does.
- A handcart, a wheelbarrow or a child's trailer moves water and firewood daily, and in a world without fuel that is more useful than a car in the drive.
- Look after feet the way you would look after an engine. Dry socks, trimmed nails, blisters covered before they burst, boots broken in long before the day you need them.
