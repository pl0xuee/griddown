More households come apart from exhaustion, boredom and resentment than from anything outside the door. Treat morale as a supply you have to produce daily, the same as water and heat.

## Structure is the treatment

- Fixed wake time, three meals however small, work blocks, and a definite end to the day. Time that isn't shaped dissolves, and people who have lost the shape of a day stop washing, then stop eating, then stop trying.
- Ritual anchors: a hot drink at the same hour, one meal eaten together with everybody sitting down, clean clothes on a set day.
- **Protect sleep above almost everything.** Seven to eight hours, dark, quiet and warm. If there's a watch rota, rotate the bad shift so nobody gets broken sleep for days running — that is how good people start making dangerous mistakes.
- Get outdoors in the morning. Daylight early stabilises sleep, and physical work in daylight beats sitting with the radio on.
- Wash. Shave or don't, but decide. Standards are load-bearing.

## What to expect, and what it means

In the first weeks most people get some of: hypervigilance, jumpiness, irritability, poor concentration, intrusive replays, vivid dreams, guilt about decisions, numbness, appetite loss. These are normal responses to abnormal events, not weakness and not illness. With sleep, food, safety and company, most of it fades.

The signs that need somebody to step in:

- Not eating or drinking, or not sleeping at all for several days.
- Withdrawing completely, or being unable to do the simplest task.
- Alcohol as the answer to every evening.
- Hopelessness, or talk of not being here.

Take talk of suicide seriously and **ask directly** — asking does not plant the idea, it usually brings relief. Don't leave the person alone, remove the obvious means, sit with them, and get them to anyone with more training than you have. Say plainly that you'll stay.

Alcohol deserves its own line: it wrecks sleep, dehydrates, dissolves judgement, and is the most common way a group takes a bad turn. Ration it deliberately or don't have it.

## Running a group

- **Brief people.** Uncertainty is worse than bad news. A ten-minute meeting each morning: what we know, what changed, what we're doing today, who's doing it. Then everybody gets a job, including the ones who don't want one.
- Conflict is coming — over work, food, noise, somebody's partner, who gets the last of the coffee. Agree in advance how decisions get made and who has the final say, and deal with grievances early and in private. Small resentments break more groups than strangers do.
- Kindness and humour are force multipliers. So is one person who cooks properly with almost nothing.
- Praise the invisible work: water carried, waste dealt with, children kept calm.

## Keeping hold of time

- Mark occasions — birthdays, Sundays, the solstice, the anniversary of something good. A calendar with things on it is how you know time is passing rather than dissolving.
- Have something to look forward to this week and something to look forward to next month.
- Keep entertainment that needs no power: cards, dice, books, an instrument, a chess set, someone who tells stories. It weighs nothing and it is genuinely the difference between a household that endures and one that grinds down.
- Name the losses out loud — people, plans, a version of the future you were entitled to expect. Grief that gets said is grief that moves.
