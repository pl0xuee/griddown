Security is mostly avoidance. The household that nobody notices, that everyone on the street knows and likes, and that has nothing visibly worth taking, has already solved most of the problem. Everything below is about staying in that position.

## Not being worth robbing

- Say nothing about what you have. Not to friends, not on the radio, not online, not to the neighbour who is only asking because he's frightened. The person known to be prepared becomes everybody else's plan.
- Rubbish is intelligence. Packaging tells anyone who looks exactly what you bought and how much of it. Burn or bury it away from the house.
- Keep the outside ordinary: no new lock glinting on a shabby door, no stack of jerrycans visible through a window, no smell of cooking meat at six o'clock when nobody else has any.
- Keep a working stock separate from your reserve, and let what's visible be modest.

## Light and noise discipline

- One lit window on a dark street is visible for a kilometre and says the power, or the fuel, is here.
- Blackout material over windows before dark — hang it, tape it, and check it from outside. Use the lowest light that works; a dimmed red headlamp is enough to move around.
- Cook when other people cook, so smoke and smell aren't singular. Run a generator in daylight and only while it earns its fuel.
- At night, sound carries several times as far. Voices, a door slam, a dog, a chainsaw, an argument — all of it maps you.

## Layers

Think in four: the neighbourhood, the boundary, the building, the room. Each layer should deter, delay and detect, and buy you time to decide.

- **Neighbourhood**: people who know your face and would knock if a stranger were at your door. Worth more than all the other layers combined.
- **Boundary**: gravel that crunches, a gate that squeaks, hedges low enough to see over, nothing to climb on, clear sightlines from a window.
- **Building**: most forced entries go through the door frame, not the lock. Long screws — 75 mm (3 in) — through the strike plate and hinges into the stud, plus a secondary bolt and a bar or wedge at night, do far more than an expensive cylinder. Film or a board on the vulnerable pane.
- **Room**: one room everybody can gather in, with a door that locks, a torch, a radio, and a way out.

## Working with neighbours

- A street that co-operates is not a target, and a street that doesn't is a collection of individually vulnerable houses.
- Agree a signal — a whistle code, a phone tree, a light in a particular window — that means "come" or "trouble here".
- Share the load: a watch rota so everybody sleeps, one cooking fire instead of six, a list of who has skills, tools, a well, a generator, and who lives alone and needs checking.
- Take the vulnerable into the arrangement rather than around it. The people you help are the ones who will be awake at three in the morning when you need them.

## If it goes wrong anyway

- Property is replaceable, and nothing in the house is worth a fight. Give it up, keep distance, break contact, withdraw to your gathering room, make noise, get help.
- Vary your routine — the time you fetch water, the route you take. Predictability is the vulnerability that most people never think about.
- Teach children what not to say, without frightening them: we don't talk about what's in our house.
- Keep documents, cash and copies somewhere separate from the obvious.

This is a defensive posture on purpose. Groups that start treating their neighbours as enemies get people killed, and become the thing they were afraid of.
