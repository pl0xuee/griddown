summary: Re-entry hurts people because the danger looks over. How to check a building, restore utilities safely, and come back into normal life.

People get injured coming home. The emergency looks finished, so caution goes, and the hazards left behind — gas, live wires, contaminated water, an unstable wall, a flooded floor and an electric drill — do the damage the disaster didn't.

## Before you go in

- If any authority is still saying "not yet", wait. Approach in daylight, never alone, and tell somebody where you are.
- Walk the outside first: leaning or bulging walls, new cracks, a sagging roofline, a shifted foundation, a leaning chimney. If it looks wrong, it is wrong — don't go in.
- Treat every downed cable as live and stay 10 m (30 ft) away, including anything it is touching, fence or puddle.
- If you smell gas or hear hissing, don't switch anything on or off, don't use a phone inside, leave, shut the supply off at the meter only if you can do it from outside, and report it.

## Restoring utilities

- Switch everything off at the consumer unit before restoring power, then bring circuits back one at a time and watch for smells and heat.
- Never touch electrical equipment while standing in water, and never restore power to a circuit that has been submerged until it has been checked.
- Before opening the stop tap, look for burst pipes; a system that froze will empty into the ceiling within minutes.
- Ventilate for an hour before you spend time inside. Torch, not a naked flame.
- Boots, gloves, long sleeves and a mask. Flood residue is sewage, mould and whatever was in the shed upstream.

## What has to go, and what doesn't

- Anything porous that got flood water and can't be washed hot: carpet, underlay, mattresses, upholstery, insulation, and plasterboard to 30–50 cm above the tide line.
- Mould establishes in 24–48 hours. Open everything, get air moving, dry aggressively, then disinfect hard surfaces with a 1:10 household bleach solution where sewage is involved.
- Throw out food that touched flood water, including screw-top jars and crimped bottle caps — neither is watertight. Tins can be saved: remove the label, wash, disinfect, relabel with a marker.
- Judge the freezer by temperature and ice crystals, not smell. Still holding ice crystals means refreezable; above 4 °C for more than a couple of hours means gone.
- Photograph everything before you clear it, keep receipts, and don't throw away the proof of what you lost.

## The other re-entry

Coming back into a working society is its own adjustment, and the flat, angry, exhausted phase usually arrives after the crisis rather than during it.

- Expect bureaucracy: ID, banking, claims, forms that assume nothing unusual happened. Start the paperwork early and keep copies.
- Expect to be out of step with people who weren't there. They will want the short version. You may not want to give it.
- Sleep, daylight, food and routine are still the medicine. So is telling somebody the whole thing once, properly.
- Watch children for weeks, not days. They process late, and they take their reading of events from the adults.

## Before you relax

Debrief the household while it's fresh: what worked, what nearly cost you, what you'd never be without again. Rebuild the water, food, fuel and medical stores now, at normal prices, in a quiet week. Then go and knock on the doors of the people who helped you.
