Barter economies don't spring up the morning after. They grow out of favours between people who already know each other, and they arrive later than the films suggest. Plan for the sequence: cards fail, cash works, favours and sharing, then trade.

## Money first

- Card networks and ATMs depend on power and connectivity; physical currency doesn't. Keep some cash at home in small denominations — nobody will have change, and a large note in a crisis buys you a small note's worth.
- Cash keeps working for weeks or months into most disruptions, and paying with it avoids every awkward negotiation below.
- Precious metals are poor short-term barter: hard to value, impossible to make change for, and an advertisement that you have more. They store value over years, not weeks.

## What people actually want

Small, consumable, hard to make, immediately useful:

- Batteries (AA, AAA, 18650), lighters and matches, candles, cordage, tarpaulins, duct tape, fixings, tools and files.
- Soap, bleach, water treatment, toothpaste, sanitary supplies, nappies.
- Salt, sugar, coffee, tea, cooking oil, spices, yeast.
- Over-the-counter painkillers, antiseptic, dressings, reading glasses.
- Seed, fuel, hand-tools, sewing supplies, boot repair.

Alcohol, tobacco and ammunition trade extremely well and bring the wrong people to your door. Ammunition in particular may be pointed back at you. Decide in advance whether you want to be known as a source; it is very hard to become unknown afterwards.

## Skills trade best

Nobody can steal a skill, it doesn't take up shelf space, and it renews. Welding, small-engine and bicycle repair, plumbing, sewing and shoe repair, butchery, food preservation, midwifery, dentistry, veterinary work, beekeeping, teaching children. Trade lessons as well as labour — the person who taught four neighbours to fix a bicycle is safer than the person who owns four bicycles.

## Rules that keep you safe

- **Never trade from your main stock.** Keep a small, separate trading pool, and let it be all anyone sees.
- Trade in a neutral place, in daylight, with someone else present, and not at your front door. Never let a trading partner see your store or count your stock.
- With a new party, trade small first, and keep the second meeting somewhere else.
- Don't be the person known for having everything. Be one of several people who have some things.
- Value is local, volatile and personal — a bag of salt may be worthless in one street and priceless in the next. Don't stockpile "barter goods" as an investment; stockpile what you'd use yourself, and trade the surplus.
- Food you'll need in month six is not surplus in month two. Do the arithmetic before you feel generous.

## Doing it properly at community scale

- A fixed market hour or day at a known place beats haggling door to door: fewer strangers wandering, more witnesses, better prices, less risk to everyone.
- A tool library, a labour exchange and a notice board move more value than any pile of trade goods.
- Keep written records of debts and promises, keep them simple, and expect to forgive some. Trying to enforce a debt in a community you depend on costs more than the debt.
- Generosity within a group you rely on is not charity, it is insurance. Trade with strangers; share with neighbours.
