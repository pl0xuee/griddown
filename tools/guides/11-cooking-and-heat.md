More people die of carbon monoxide and house fires after a power cut than of cold. Read the first section before you light anything.

## Carbon monoxide, first and last

- **Never burn charcoal indoors.** Not in a fireplace, not in a garage, not in a tent, not in an open doorway. A barbecue in a room kills a family overnight, and it is the single most common way people die in a blackout.
- Never run a generator indoors, in a garage, in a basement or within 6 m (20 ft) of a window, door or vent — including the neighbour's.
- Camping stoves are for outdoors or a genuinely ventilated space, and for cooking only, never heating.
- Unvented paraffin or catalytic heaters need a window open and an oxygen depletion sensor. They also dump a lot of water vapour into the room; expect condensation and mould.
- CO has no smell. Get a **battery carbon monoxide alarm** and put it where people sleep. Symptoms are headache, nausea, dizziness and confusion in more than one person at once, better when outdoors — if that happens, get everyone out into fresh air immediately and don't go back in.

## Burn less fuel

- Lid on the pot. A lid alone can halve the fuel to boil.
- A windshield outdoors saves more than any stove upgrade.
- Simmer, don't boil hard. A rolling boil is not hotter, just faster to empty.
- **Retained-heat cooking**: bring grain or stew to a rolling boil for 10–15 minutes, then seal the pot in a well-insulated box — a haybox, a duvet, a sleeping bag in a crate — for 2–4 hours. It saves 50–80% of the fuel and can't burn the food.
- **Red kidney beans are the exception, and it matters.** They carry a toxin that a long warm soak makes *worse* rather than better — held around 80 °C, which is exactly what a haybox does, they can end up several times more poisonous than raw. Soak them at least five hours, **throw the soaking water away**, then boil hard for a full 30 minutes in fresh water before they go anywhere near an insulated box. Cannellini and broad beans want the same treatment. Lentils, split peas and chickpeas do not.
- A pressure cooker turns a two-hour pot of beans into 30 minutes and pays for its weight in fuel within a week.
- Soak dried beans and grains overnight; it cuts cooking time by a third or more.
- Cook once a day for the whole household. One fire, one pot, less smoke and less smell for anyone to notice.
- Solar cookers work in clear sun, reaching 90–150 °C. Slow, silent, free, and useless under cloud.

## Heating

- Heat the person, then the room, then never the house. Layers, hats indoors, insulation under you as well as over you — the floor and the mattress steal more heat than the air.
- Choose one room, preferably small, south-facing and away from the wind. Close doors, hang blankets over doorways and windows at night, block draughts at the bottom of doors, and put everyone in it.
- Sleep together and sleep off the floor. Two people under shared blankets need far less fuel than two people in separate rooms.
- A "flowerpot candle heater" is a myth. One candle is about 80 watts; it will not heat a room, but it will burn the house down if you leave it.

## Wood

- Burn seasoned wood at 20% moisture or less. Wet wood gives half the heat and coats the flue in creosote, which is how houses burn down in month two. Sweep the flue.
- Never burn treated, painted or pressure-treated timber, chipboard or driftwood. The fumes are genuinely poisonous and the salt in driftwood destroys the stove.
- Plan roughly 3–5 cords (11–18 m³) for a full northern winter of whole-house heating, or 1–2 cords to keep one room going. Cut it a year ahead — this winter's wood should already be dry.
- Keep a fire extinguisher and a lid to smother a pan fire. Never put water on burning fat or oil; the lid or a damp cloth, and turn the heat off.
