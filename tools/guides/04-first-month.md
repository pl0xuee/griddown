The first month is where optimism meets arithmetic. Consumption is now measurable, the novelty has gone, and whatever you improvised in week one either works or has quietly started to fail.

## Recalculate against real consumption

- You now know what you actually burn, drink and eat per day. Redo every projection with those numbers, not the ones you assumed.
- Write the run-out dates somewhere everyone can see: food, cooking fuel, heating fuel, water treatment, batteries, each person's medication, soap, sanitary supplies.
- Start substituting before you are forced to. Stretch meals with grain and pulses. Keep one proper hot meal a day as a fixed point.

## Nutrition, not just calories

Calories keep you alive; the rest keeps you working.

- **Fat** runs out first and is hardest to replace. Ration cooking oil harder than you want to.
- **Protein**: pulses, tinned fish, eggs, nuts. Weakness and slow-healing wounds are the early signs of running short.
- **Vitamin C**: deficiency shows at roughly one to three months without any — bleeding gums, old wounds reopening, exhaustion. Potatoes, fresh greens, rosehips and sprouted pulses all supply it, and sprouting a jar of dried beans or lentils takes three days and no equipment. Two warnings on that list. **Cook sprouted beans** — raw sprouted kidney beans in particular still carry the toxin that makes raw beans dangerous, and raw sprouts of any kind are among the most outbreak-prone foods there are, which matters more when the water is doubtful. And **pine needle tea is only safe if you are certain what tree it came from**: yew is planted around houses, churchyards and car parks everywhere, its needles look near enough, and it stops the heart with little warning and no antidote. Yew needles are flat and soft with a pale green underside and grow in two ranks off the twig; pines carry theirs in bundles of two to five in a papery sheath. If you cannot tell, do not make the tea. Pine needle tea is also avoided in pregnancy.
- A daily multivitamin is trivial to store and covers most of this. Iodised salt covers another gap.

## Fix what week one improvised

- Sanitation review: is the pit filling faster than expected, is soap still at the handwashing point, has anybody been ill and why.
- Maintenance: sharpen tools, sweep the flue (creosote is how houses burn down in month two), scrub water containers, repair boots.
- Health: look at the things nobody mentions — a tooth, a cut that isn't closing, weight loss, feet, a persistent cough. Weigh people if you have scales.
- Security: your routine is now set, which means it is predictable. Vary times. Walk outside after dark and look at your own house the way a stranger would.

## Move from neighbours to community

- Organise what was ad hoc: shared cooking, shared childcare, a skills list, a notice board, a fixed market or meeting hour.
- Shared watch means everybody sleeps. Shared childcare frees the strongest labour. Shared meals cut fuel by more than half.
- Agree simple rules while everyone is still reasonable — how decisions get made, how disputes get settled, what happens to people who arrive.

## Resupply and the season ahead

- Work out what can be legally scavenged, foraged, grown, fished, hunted or traded for. Find out what the local rules actually are; assuming they've lapsed is how people get hurt or arrested.
- Plan the next season now: winter fuel cut and stacked, spring seed set aside, storage prepared for a harvest you don't have yet.
- Get children into a structure of lessons and jobs. It is schooling and it is morale.

## Expect the flatness

Month one is where adrenaline drains and the grey arrives — irritability, poor sleep, no appetite, nothing to look forward to. That is a normal response, not a failure. Structure, daylight, physical work and a fixed occasion each week are the treatment. See the morale guide.
