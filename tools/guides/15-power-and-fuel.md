Most of what you'd like to power, you don't need. Start by separating the loads that keep somebody alive from the ones that keep you comfortable, and size for the first list.

## What actually needs power

- **Life-critical**: oxygen concentrator, CPAP, a fridge holding insulin, a powered wheelchair, a well pump.
- **High value, tiny draw**: LED lighting, a radio, torches, charging phones and power banks.
- **Comfort**: everything else. Heating and cooking by electricity is off the table on any small system — resistive heat eats a battery bank in an hour.

## Do the arithmetic in watt-hours

- Phone charge: about 15 Wh. LED lamp: 5 W, so 50 Wh for a ten-hour night across a few lamps. Laptop: 50 W. CPAP: 30–60 W, so 300–500 Wh a night. A domestic fridge: 1–2 kWh a day, which is a serious system on its own.
- A 100 W solar panel makes roughly 300–500 Wh on a clear summer day. In midwinter at a northern latitude under cloud, assume a quarter of that.
- A 12 V 100 Ah lead-acid battery holds 1.2 kWh nominal, but only about half is usable without wrecking it — call it 600 Wh. LiFePO4 of the same rating gives you most of its capacity and thousands of cycles rather than hundreds.
- Running loads directly at 12 V avoids the 10–15% an inverter wastes, so USB and 12 V lighting are worth building around.

## Generators

- Size it to charge batteries and run the fridge for a couple of hours, then switch it off. Running a generator continuously to power a lamp burns a tank a day.
- Fuel burn is roughly 0.5–1 litre an hour for a 2 kW inverter set at half load.
- **Outdoors only** — never in a garage, porch, basement or within 6 m (20 ft) of any window, door or vent, yours or a neighbour's. Carbon monoxide from generators kills people every storm season.
- **Never backfeed** through a wall socket. It can electrocute line workers and start a fire. A transfer switch or interlock fitted by an electrician is the only safe way to feed a house.
- A generator is audible for a kilometre and tells everybody who has power. Run it in daylight, when other noise exists, and only when it's earning its fuel.

## Storing fuel

- Petrol goes stale in 3–6 months, up to about a year with stabiliser. Diesel keeps 6–12 months and longer with a biocide — watch for water and microbial growth in the tank. Propane and butane keep indefinitely and are the easiest fuels to store safely.
- Approved containers only, clearly labelled, out of the living space, in the shade, away from any pilot light or ignition source. Check your local limits on domestic quantities; 25–30 litres is a common cap.
- Petrol vapour is heavier than air and explosive. Never store it in a basement, under stairs, or anywhere vapour can pool and find a flame.
- Rotate: put stored fuel into the car and refill the can, on a date you write down.

## Batteries and small power

- Keep lead-acid batteries charged; left flat they sulphate dead within weeks. Store lithium cells at around half charge, out of the cold.
- Rechargeable AA cells and a small solar charger run headlamps and a radio essentially forever, at a fraction of the cost of alkalines.
- Charge every power bank in the first hour of an outage, and keep one untouched as a reserve for communications.
- A car with an inverter is a generator you already own — 150–300 W from the accessory socket, more from a fused connection at the battery. The battery alone flattens in an hour or two and then the car won't start, so run the engine to charge, outdoors.
- Fuse everything, use the right cable size, and keep a torch where you can find it in the dark.
