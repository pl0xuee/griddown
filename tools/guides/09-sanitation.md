In every large emergency the killer on the record is the same: diarrhoeal disease, spread from faeces to mouths by way of hands, flies, food and water. It takes small children and old people first, and it is almost entirely preventable with buckets, soap and discipline. Give it the same weight as water and food.

## The rule

Human waste never touches hands, food, water or flies. Every arrangement below is just a way of enforcing that.

## Where it goes

- **Sewer still working, water available**: keep flushing. A normal toilet takes about 6–9 litres of grey water poured in fast. Stop instantly if drains gurgle, back up or smell — you are downstream of something that has failed, and a backed-up sewer inside the house is a disaster of its own.
- **Bucket system, indoors**: two lined buckets with lids. Urine in one, faeces and paper in the other, covered after each use with a scoop of sawdust, dry soil, leaves, shredded paper or wood ash. The cover material is what stops the smell and the flies; keep more of it than you think you need.
- **Pit or trench latrine, outdoors**: at least **30 m (100 ft)** from any well, spring, stream or water store, and downhill of it, with the pit floor at least 1.5–2 m above the water table. Dig it deep and narrow — 30 cm wide, a metre or more deep — with a solid cover, a slab either side to stand on, and a mound and channel so rain runs away rather than in. Allow roughly 60–90 litres of pit volume per person per year, and site the replacement before the first is full.
- **One person, moving**: a cat hole 15–20 cm (6–8 in) deep, at least 60 m (200 ft) from water, camp and trail, and filled in.
- **Urine** is far less of an infection risk than faeces — though not sterile; typhoid, leptospirosis and schistosomiasis can all travel in it — and is mostly a volume problem: 1–2 litres per person per day. Keep it out of the solids — separating the two roughly halves the smell and the fly problem. Diluted about 1:10 with water it is a good nitrogen feed for fruit trees and non-leaf crops; don't put it on anything you eat raw.

## Handwashing is the intervention

Handwashing with soap cuts diarrhoeal disease by around a third — no other single thing you do comes close.

- Put a washing point immediately outside the toilet and a second where food is prepared. A tippy-tap — a hung jerrycan tipped by a foot rope, soap on a string — uses a fraction of a litre per wash and nobody touches a tap.
- Wash after the toilet, after nappies or a sick person, after waste or animals, before preparing food, before eating.
- Twenty seconds with soap, under the nails included. Soap matters far more than the volume of water. Alcohol gel of 60% or more is a backup only — it fails on visibly dirty hands and does nothing against norovirus or Cryptosporidium.

## When somebody has diarrhoea

- Rehydrate immediately: **1 litre of clean water, 6 level teaspoons of sugar, half a level teaspoon of salt**. Small sips, continuously. An adult losing fluid fast may need 3 litres or more a day; keep giving it even if they vomit. Never make it saltier than the recipe.
- Get help if there is blood in the stool, a stiff or sunken look with no urine for 8 hours or more, confusion, or a small child who will not drink.
- Isolate: their own bucket, towel and cup. One person cares for them, and that person washes.
- Disinfect with household bleach: **1:10** for vomit, faeces and the toilet itself; **1:100** for floors, handles and general surfaces. Leave it wet on the surface for at least a minute. Mix fresh daily — diluted bleach loses strength within about 24 hours.
- Boil, don't just chlorinate, drinking water for the household until it has passed.

## The rest of the chain

- Flies breed in exposed waste and land on food. Lids and covers on everything, and food kept under cloth or mesh.
- Nappies and children's faeces are the highest-risk material in any household. Bag them, cover them, wash like it matters.
- Greywater to a rubble-filled soakaway well away from the kitchen and any drinking source. Never let it stand.
- Rubbish and food scraps buried or burned away from the house; they bring rats.
- Menstrual supplies: stock more than you think, plus soap, a covered bucket for washables and somewhere sunny to dry them.
- Burying the dead: at least 30 m from any water source, 1.5 m above the water table, and marked. The infection risk from bodies is usually overstated, but it is real for cholera and haemorrhagic illness — gloves, and wash afterwards.
