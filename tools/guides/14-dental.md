Toothache is one of the most common serious problems in any long emergency, and dentistry is the one field where doing nothing reliably makes things worse. A tooth that hurts today is an abscess in a fortnight. Get outstanding dental work done now, while there are dentists.

## Prevention is the whole game

- Brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste and clean between the teeth. Two minutes, properly, beats anything you can do later.
- Stock spare brushes, paste and floss — they are light, cheap and last for years. A brush should be replaced every three months or so.
- If paste runs out: brushing with plain water still removes plaque. Bicarbonate of soda works as a dentifrice. A salt rinse — half a teaspoon in 250 ml of warm water — soothes gums but does not clean teeth.
- Sugar is the driver. In an emergency diet heavy on sweetened drinks, jam and boiled sweets, expect decay to accelerate sharply, especially in children.

## Temporary repairs

- **Lost filling or broken tooth**: clean the cavity, dry it, and pack it with over-the-counter temporary filling material (zinc oxide and eugenol), then bite gently to seat it. Sugar-free gum is a stopgap for an hour, not a week. A packet of temporary filling material costs almost nothing and belongs in the kit.
- **Knocked-out adult tooth**: hold it by the crown, never the root. Rinse briefly in milk or saline if dirty — don't scrub it and don't let it dry. Put it straight back in the socket and have the person bite on a clean cloth. If that isn't possible, keep it in milk or the person's own saliva, never water, and reach a dentist within 30–60 minutes. Baby teeth are never put back.
- **Broken orthodontic wire**: bend it flat, or cover the end with orthodontic wax or sugar-free gum. **Denture sore**: stop wearing it and rinse with salt water until the ulcer heals.

## Toothache

- Over-the-counter painkillers taken to the label. For most adults who can take both, paracetamol (acetaminophen) and ibuprofen alternated control dental pain better than either alone.
- Clove oil (eugenol) on a scrap of cotton wool held against the tooth gives real relief. Keep it off the gum and tongue, where it burns.
- Cold compress on the outside of the cheek. Never heat over a swollen face — warmth spreads infection.
- Sleep propped up; lying flat increases the throbbing. Keep temperature extremes and sugar off that side.

## The emergency

A dental abscess can become life-threatening when it spreads into the floor of the mouth or the neck. Treat these as an emergency needing a clinician and antibiotics, immediately:

- Swelling spreading to the eye, the jawline or the neck.
- Fever with facial swelling.
- Difficulty swallowing, opening the mouth, or breathing, or a raised tongue.

If there is genuinely no help available, warm salt-water rinses several times an hour can encourage a pointing abscess to drain into the mouth, which relieves pressure. Keep fluids going, keep the person upright, and keep watching the airway. Be clear-eyed: this is a holding action, not a treatment.

## Don't attempt an extraction

Without training, instruments and anaesthetic, the usual results are a fractured root left in the jaw, bleeding you can't stop, and a worse infection than the one you started with. It is almost always better to manage pain and infection and wait for someone who can do it properly.

If a tooth is already lost or a socket is bleeding: bite firmly on rolled gauze for 20–30 minutes, don't rinse, spit or use a straw for 24 hours, and expect a spike of severe pain around day three to five if the clot is lost — salt rinses and painkillers, and keep it clean.
