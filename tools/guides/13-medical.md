First aid handles the first ten minutes. This is about the weeks after: the medicines somebody takes every day, wounds that turn, illness that doesn't pass, and knowing which problems you genuinely cannot solve at home.

## Prevention is nearly all of it

Clean water, handwashing with soap, safe food, working sanitation and enough sleep prevent more illness than any kit you can buy. Beyond that: keep vaccinations current, get a tetanus booster if yours is over ten years old, and get the medical and dental work you've been putting off done while services exist.

## Chronic medication is the first crisis

- Write a list, now, for every person: drug, dose, what it's for, prescriber. Photograph the labels. That list is worth more than anything in a first aid kit.
- Count days of supply. Ask your pharmacist or doctor about a longer supply for emergencies; many will agree if you explain why.
- **Some drugs are dangerous to stop abruptly** — beta blockers, steroids, anti-epileptics, benzodiazepines, opioids, some antidepressants. Rebound, seizures and adrenal crisis are real risks. Find out which of yours are in that group while you can still ask.
- Insulin: unopened vials keep at 2–8 °C; in use, most keep about 28 days at room temperature. Heat above 30 °C degrades it. A cellar, an evaporative cooler or an insulated box with cool water buys weeks.
- Oxygen, dialysis and CPAP make somebody dependent on power or on a delivery. That is a plan you make on the first day, not in the second week.
- No dosing tables here for prescription drugs. Keep the label and the patient leaflet, don't improvise doses, and don't share medicines between people.

## Infection

Recognise it early, because untreated infection is the classic quiet killer.

- Local signs: increasing pain after day two, spreading redness, heat, swelling, pus, red streaks running from the wound, a wound that reopens.
- Systemic signs: fever, shivering, fast breathing, fast heart rate, confusion, feeling far worse than the injury explains. That combination is sepsis. It needs a hospital, and there is no home treatment for it.
- Wounds: irrigate hard with plenty of clean water — 250–500 ml squeezed from a bottle is not too much — and get the grit out. Don't close a dirty wound, a puncture, a bite or anything more than a few hours old: pack it, dress it, let it heal from the bottom, and look at it every day when you change the dressing.
- Antibiotics: partial courses breed resistance, most sore throats and coughs are viral, and treating blind usually treats the wrong thing. If you'll be genuinely remote, ask a clinician about a standby course and the conditions for using it. That is a medical conversation, not a stockpiling exercise.

## Things you should know cold

- **Burns**: cool under running water for 20 minutes, within three hours of the injury. No ice, no butter. Cover with cling film laid on, not wrapped. Anything bigger than the patient's palm, or on face, hands, feet or genitals, or right around a limb, or white and leathery, needs a hospital.
- **Dehydration** kills more people than the illness that caused it. Oral rehydration solution: 1 litre clean water, 6 level teaspoons sugar, half a level teaspoon salt, sipped continuously.
- **Fractures**: splint including the joint above and below, elevate, and check fingers or toes for warmth, colour and feeling before and after. An open fracture, a deformed limb, or a limb that goes cold or numb needs a surgeon.
- **Chest pain, stroke signs, a rigid painful abdomen, a head injury with worsening drowsiness, difficulty breathing**: all need a hospital. If there isn't one, keep them warm, still and hydrated if awake, write down times and observations, and say plainly what you can and cannot do.
- Childbirth almost always proceeds by itself. Keep the baby warm, dry and skin-to-skin, don't pull, and watch the mother for heavy bleeding.

Finally: do a real first aid or first responder course. Two days in a room with an instructor is worth more than every guide you will ever read, including this one.
